My grandfather is not a man who likes to talk much. In fact, he hardly speaks unless he grunts happily over a pork chop or asks someone to change the TV back to golf. My mother says he just chooses his words carefully; I think he is simply an unpleasant old man.

He has always been like that. Even when I was a cute little toddler walking unsteadily around his living room, he barely noticed me. He just sat in his soft armchair reading the newspaper, ignoring my happy cries as I practiced dancing in front of him.

“Grandpa, Grandpa, look at me!” I would call out to him. He would only lift his newspaper higher to block me from his view.

Only my grandmother gave me the love and comfort I wanted from a grandparent. Throughout my childhood, she spoiled me: pinching my cheeks, baking cookies for me, and praising every little sound or achievement. So when she died last spring, I was heartbroken. My grandfather seemed heartbroken too.

That was when my mother came up with her plan for the two of us to get closer. She sent me to live with him for two weeks during summer vacation while she went on her honeymoon with her new husband. Although I was fifteen and perfectly able to stay home alone for two weeks, my mother wanted to give my grandfather a chance to show me the love he should have shown me since the day I was born.

“You'll have fun, sweetheart,” my mother said sincerely as she almost pushed me out of the still-moving car.

“He doesn't even talk!” I shouted in frustration.

“He does talk.” My mother rolled her eyes. “You just have to listen.”

The first week and a half went almost exactly as I expected. We ignored each other in silence and ate our meals separately: he ate in front of the TV, and I ate in the guest bedroom. On the final night of my visit, I woke up in the middle of the night to use the bathroom and noticed a light coming from his bedroom. Curious, I peeked around the corner. My grandfather was sitting in his armchair with a glass of whisky in his hand and eyes swollen from crying. He was staring at the air vent beside his bed.

“I wish you were still here,” he whispered. I could barely hear him over the sound of ice hitting the glass.

“Who do you wish were still here?” I asked softly as I stepped closer. He looked up at me and motioned for me to sit on the edge of the bed. I sat nervously on the edge and looked at him. I don't think we had ever sat so close together before.

Without saying anything, he handed me the glass of whisky. I took a sip, and the sharp taste burned my tongue and sent a shiver down my back. Then I handed the glass back to him.

“I think it's time for you to learn about my sister,” he said quietly.

What follows is his story, told in his own words.

I know you don't think very well of me; I don't think very well of myself either. To tell the truth, there are many things I would change if I could. You are still young, but one day you will understand.

My parents and I have lived in this house since I was a little boy. Back then, after the war, this place seemed like a castle to me. I loved living here. Believe it or not, this room used to be mine. To an eight-year-old boy, it was his kingdom.

I used to pretend my mother and father were the king and queen, and I was a prince. I ruled my stuffed animals as if they were my subjects. My parents even encouraged the game because they thought it was cute. My mother was a homemaker. In those days, most women were. So she was usually home to cook, clean, and play children's games with me. My father was different. He paid attention to me when he was home, but he was rarely home. You see, my father was a preacher, a “man of faith.”

When you are young, you simply trust your parents. You trust their words, believe what they say, and have no reason to doubt them. So when they told me to go to church three times a week, I obeyed. It was fun being the preacher's son. Wherever we went, my mother and I seemed to be surrounded by a holy glow. Everyone saw us as the perfect family, a family of faith, God, and goodness. My father was known as a man of God, someone the whole community could trust. So when my father told me not to pay attention to the sounds coming from the attic, I obeyed him.

I first noticed those sounds on the day we moved into the house. I was asleep when I heard a muffled cry coming through the air vent. The cry stopped at once after a dull thud. I immediately fell asleep again. During the next few weeks, I sometimes heard little footsteps or strange thuds in the middle of the night. My father told me they were rats. Like most children, I gradually got used to the occasional noises.

Then one night, I was still playing in my room when I should have been sleeping. I held my toy sword up before my stuffed animals and pretended I was making them knights. I was telling them to bow before me when I heard a voice.

“Hello?”

The voice was quiet and muffled, like an echo, and I could barely hear it. It was a little girl's voice. I think that is why I paid so much attention. I was not allowed to bring girls into the house.

“Is someone there?” the voice called again. Then I realized it was coming from the air vent beside my bed. I quickly crawled toward it, letting my toy sword fall noisily onto the floor.

“Who's there?” I asked in the bravest voice I could manage.

“I'm Polly. I live in the attic.” Then I noticed how young and strained her voice sounded. I moved my head closer to the vent.

“Are you my brother?” the voice asked.

“I'm not sure. Are you my sister?” I asked back.

“I don't know. Daddy says I have a brother, but he won't let me see him.”

“Why won't he?”

“Daddy says there's something wrong with me.”

“Is that true?”

“I'm not very good at thinking.”

“Oh. Who is your father?” I asked.

“Michael Larson.”

“That's my father too!” I shouted excitedly. “Then you must be my sister, because we have the same father.”

“If we have the same father, are we brother and sister?”

“I think so, but to be honest, I'm not completely sure.”

“I don't know either.” We were quiet for a while.

“Why do you live in the attic?” I finally asked the question I had been wanting to ask.

“Daddy says I can't go outside because I'm not like other people.”

“So you've never been outside?”

“I don't think so,” Polly answered nervously. “I'm not allowed to talk about it.”

“Oh.” I moved back on the bed, confused. “I'm... um, going to sleep now,” I said hesitantly into the vent.

“Oh, okay,” Polly answered. Her voice broke, and for a moment I thought she was about to cry.

“But... but I'll come back!” I said quickly to comfort her.

“Really?”

“Of course I'm your brother,” I promised her.

“And I'm your sister.”

The next morning, I woke up feeling very excited. Not only was it Saturday, but I also had a little sister! I hurried down the stairs, taking two steps at a time, looking forward to breakfast. As on every Saturday, my mother had prepared a large breakfast. After my father led the morning prayer, I eagerly ate the hot pancakes and sausages on my plate.

“Hey, hey, slow down, little fellow,” my father laughed as he watched me stuff food into my mouth. “Why are you in such a hurry?”

“I want to finish eating quickly so I can go play with my little sister,” I explained while chewing a piece of toast.

My mother's face turned pale gray, as if she had seen a ghost. My father clenched his jaw and very carefully set down his knife and fork. Neither of them said a word.

“MJ,” my father said, unable to hide the harshness in his voice. “You do not have a sister.”

I looked up from my plate, confused. “But I do, Dad; she lives in—”

“ENOUGH!” My father roared and slammed his fist on the table. My mother was looking down at her hands, which were folded neatly in her lap. I saw a tear fall onto her plate.

“Can't you see that your lies are upsetting your mother?” he hissed.

“But... but I'm not—”

“Go to your room right now, MJ,” my father ordered. “If you dare lie to us again, I promise I will never let you leave your room.”

I pushed back my chair and ran from the table, hot tears streaming down my face. I threw myself onto my bed and cried because it all felt so unfair. A few minutes later, I heard her voice.

“Are you okay?” Polly asked.

Anger suddenly filled my chest. “No!” I said bitterly. “I'm being punished, and it's all your fault!”

“What happened?” I could hear the worry in her voice, but at that moment I didn't care.

“I told my father I had a sister, and he shouted at me and punished me by making me stay inside!” I said between sobs.

“You told Daddy that we talked?” she asked in fear.

“More or less.”

“You shouldn't have done that,” she said in a trembling voice. “I'm going to be in big trouble. Daddy will... will kill me.”

“Well, you deserve it!” I screamed into the air vent. “You ruined my whole day! Everything was fine before I met you.”

Then I heard Polly crying through the air vent. I thought her muffled sobs would make me feel better, but they only made me feel worse. A feeling of guilt grew inside me. I pulled a pillow over my head so I would not hear her crying.

I must have fallen asleep, because when I woke up, the pillow was on the floor and I heard Polly again. But this time she was not alone.

“No, no,” she whimpered.

“How did you find a way to talk to him?” a voice hissed. I immediately sat up, my heart beating loudly. I recognized that voice.

“No, no, I'll be good. I'll be good,” Polly cried. I heard a thud, and then Polly cried out in pain. I pressed my ear closer to the vent. “I haven't talked to anyone.”

“You're lying!” my father shouted.

“No, I'm good. I don't lie.” I heard another thud, and now Polly was crying loudly. I trembled as I listened to what was happening above my head.

“You had better be my good little girl,” my father said. “You know what happens when you are bad.”

“Please, Daddy, please don't. I'm very, very good.”

“Take your clothes off.”

“No, no, I'm not lying—” Polly's voice was cut off by another slap. I heard her cry out and had to cover my mouth so I would not scream too.

“You heard what I said,” my father said harshly. “Take your clothes off.”

I ran out of my bedroom and all the way downstairs. Part of me desperately hoped to see my father sitting in the living room when I turned the corner. I prayed, I begged God to let me see my father sitting in his favorite chair. I prayed that I was only imagining everything and that my imagination had gone too far. But when I entered the living room, I saw only my mother sitting stiffly on the sofa, knitting.

“Mother,” I said, with tears still running down my face. “Mother, where is Father?” My whole body was shaking.

My mother looked up at me with a sad smile. “He's praying, sweetheart.”

“Are you telling the truth?”

“Of course, sweetheart,” she answered. But her eyes said something different. “Come on,” she said, “let's listen to the radio. Your favorite program should be starting soon.”

I sat down beside her, and she turned on the radio. She hummed softly as she continued knitting, pressing her lips tightly together. She held the knitting needles so tightly that her fingertips turned white.

“I love your father,” she said.

“I know, Mother, I know.”

My father's knife moved through the steak as easily as through butter. The meat was still bleeding a little, exactly how he liked it.

“MJ, have you stopped lying?” he asked without looking up from his plate.

“Yes, sir,” I answered. “I let my imagination go too far.”

My father smiled and pointed his knife toward my mother. “And what do you want to say to the woman who gave birth to you?”

My face turned red. “I'm sorry, Mother.”

“That's my good boy,” he said, continuing to eat. “So you've learned your lesson, haven't you?”

“Yes, sir.”

The conversation at dinner turned to the church fundraiser the following weekend. My mother promised to bake her famous pecan pie, while my father discussed which people from the Bible study group would attend. After dinner, I asked to go back to my room.

“Polly?” I whispered into the air vent. I heard a few small sniffs, as if she were still crying. Guilt filled my chest again.

“Polly, I'm sorry,” I murmured. “Can you forgive me?”

“I guess so,” Polly answered.

“Are you okay?”

“No.”

I looked around the room for a moment. “Well, when I'm sad, I like to play a game,” I explained as I picked up my toy sword. “Do you want to play with me?”

“Okay.”

“Okay, I'll be the prince, and you can be the princess. We can each have a kingdom to rule. Your kingdom will be the attic, and mine will be this bedroom. Does that sound fun?”

I heard her sniff. “Can I be a princess?”

“Of course!” I promised her. “You can be anyone you want to be.”

And that was how it all began: with a game. Every night, we waited until I heard my father go to sleep, and then we used our secret code. I tapped twice on the inside of the air vent; if Polly was able to talk, she tapped twice in return. Then we knew it was safe to play. Some nights, we ruled our kingdoms; on other nights, I read stories to her from a book. Some nights we just talked. It felt good to have her there. I slowly got used to our routine. But like every routine, sometimes it was interrupted.

Sometimes I tapped on the air vent and heard no answer, only strange groans and an occasional thud. Whenever I went downstairs, I saw my mother silently knitting in the living room, her knuckles white. My father was nowhere to be seen. That was how I knew he was in the attic with Polly. After those times, Polly usually did not want to talk much.

“What does the outside look like?” Polly asked one night. I was lying on my back in bed, with my head turned toward the air vent. I thought for a moment.

“Um... it's very big, I guess,” I answered awkwardly. “But it's cold now, because Christmas is coming soon. There is a lot of snow.”

“What is snow like?”

“You've never seen snow?”

“No.”

“Well,” I said, “why don't I show you?”

Polly hesitated. “I'm never allowed to leave the attic.”

I sat up in bed. “But what if you went outside just once? I can come and get you. I'll take you outside to see the snow, and then I'll bring you back! Daddy doesn't have to know.”

“Like... like a secret?” Polly asked. I could hear the excitement in her voice. “Outside! Outside!” she cried, forgetting that we usually had to speak quietly.

I laughed. “Yes, let's do that!” I shouted, carried away by the excitement.

“When?!” Polly shouted.

“We could go right—”

“Who are you talking to?” my father suddenly interrupted. My face turned bright red when I turned away from the vent to face him. I had been caught.

“N-no... no one,” I stammered weakly. “I'm just playing a game.” My father's eyes moved toward the corner of the room, where my stuffed animals had been pushed aside and forgotten.

“Playing with whom?” he demanded.

“No one, sir. Just by myself.”

My father's face became cold and hard. He gave a short, rough nod. “Then keep playing. Just try not to make too much noise.” Then he turned and walked out. I was so relieved that he believed me that I almost wet myself. I waited a few more minutes before speaking again.

“Let's wait until Daddy goes to sleep, and then I'll take you outside!” I whispered.

But there was no reply. “Polly?” I called a few minutes later. “Polly, do you still want to go outside?”

“No,” my father's voice answered. “She doesn't.” I covered my mouth with both hands and jumped up from my bed.

“MJ,” Polly called weakly. “Help me.”

But I stood frozen. I did not move when I heard thud after thud after thud. I did not move when Polly cried out for her brother. I did not move when I heard my father hit her. And I still did not move when the attic became as silent as the snow outside.

I stood in the middle of my room, with both hands tightly clenched into fists beside me. I kept standing there as I heard my father's heavy, unsteady footsteps leaving the attic. I kept standing there when the porch light suddenly came on outside the window. And I kept standing there, watching my father dig and dig and dig all night long.

In the morning, there was a small mound of newly turned dirt under the maple tree in the back yard, without snow on it like the rest of the yard. And the air vent? It was completely silent.

My grandfather set down his glass and looked toward the air vent again. The air in the room suddenly felt heavier.

“Her... her name was Polly?” I asked.

He nodded. “The same as yours.” He smiled sadly. “When you were born, I asked your mother to name you Polly.”

“Does my mother know why?”

“She knows I had a sister. But she doesn't know much more than that.”

I was quiet for a moment. “After... after that happened, what did you do?”

My grandfather looked down at his glass. “Nothing. Just as I did nothing while it was happening.”

“Was there something wrong with her?” I asked slowly. “Why... why did your father lock her away?”

He was silent for a while, then took a sip of his drink. “I think she had Down syndrome. And I think my father was ashamed of that.” He sighed.

“Grandpa, I'm so—”

“Don't,” he interrupted. “There's no need.” We sat quietly for a few minutes, listening to the soft hum of the air vent.

“Polly would have been a wonderful princess,” I whispered.

He smiled, and for a moment I felt as though I could see a glimpse of the eight-year-old boy he had once been. “Yes,” he agreed. “She would have been.”
