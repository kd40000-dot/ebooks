My grandfather is not a man who likes to talk much. In fact, he hardly speaks unless he grunts happily over a pork chop or asks someone to change the TV back to golf. My mother says he just chooses his words carefully; I think he is simply an unpleasant old man.

He has always been like that. Even when I was a cute little toddler walking unsteadily around his living room, he barely noticed me. He just sat in his soft armchair reading the newspaper, ignoring my happy cries as I practiced dancing in front of him.

“Grandpa, Grandpa, look at me!” I would call out to him. He would only lift his newspaper higher to block me from his view.

Only my grandmother gave me the love and comfort I wanted from a grandparent. Throughout my childhood, she spoiled me: pinching my cheeks, baking cookies for me, and praising every little sound or achievement. So when she died last spring, I was heartbroken. My grandfather seemed heartbroken too.

That was when my mother came up with her plan for the two of us to get closer. She sent me to live with him for two weeks during summer vacation while she went on her honeymoon with her new husband. Although I was fifteen and perfectly able to stay home alone for two weeks, my mother wanted to give my grandfather a chance to show me the love he should have shown me since the day I was born.

“You'll have fun, sweetheart,” my mother said sincerely as she almost pushed me out of the still-moving car.

“He doesn't even talk!” I shouted in frustration.

“He does talk.” My mother rolled her eyes. “You just have to listen.”

The first week and a half went almost exactly as I expected. We ignored each other in silence and ate our meals separately: he ate in front of the TV, and I ate in the guest bedroom. On the final night of my visit, I woke up in the middle of the night to use the bathroom and noticed a light coming from his bedroom. Curious, I peeked around the corner. My grandfather was sitting in his armchair with a glass of whisky in his hand and eyes swollen from crying. He was staring at the air vent beside his bed.

“I wish you were still here,” he whispered. I could barely hear him over the sound of ice hitting the glass.

“Who do you wish were still here?” I asked softly as I stepped closer. He looked up at me and motioned for me to sit on the edge of the bed. I sat nervously on the edge and looked at him. I don't think we had ever sat so close together before.

Without saying anything, he handed me the glass of whisky. I took a sip, and the sharp taste burned my tongue and sent a shiver down my back. Then I handed the glass back to him.

“I think it's time for you to learn about my sister,” he said quietly.
